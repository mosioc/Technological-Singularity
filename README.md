## Abstract

The emergence of large language models (LLMs), which have brought artificial intelligence closer to the general public, has attracted considerable attention to both the field’s rapid progress and its potential adverse consequences. Consequently, anticipating what might happen in an era when computers and their behavior surpass human intelligence and understanding has become increasingly important.

This article offers a brief examination of the technological singularity, a hypothetical future in which intelligent machines may become so advanced that their behavior can no longer be reliably predicted by humans. The concept has a long intellectual history, with John von Neumann introducing the idea of a singularity in this context, followed by contributions from Vernor Vinge, Ray Kurzweil, and David J. Chalmers. Drawing on their work, this article examines the evidence for the singularity, the factors that could accelerate or impede its emergence, and its potential consequences once it occurs.

## 1. Introduction

Ray Kurzweil and several other thinkers expect artificial intelligence (AI) to surpass human intelligence substantially during the twenty-first century. Since Isaac Asimov introduced his Three Laws of Robotics in 1942, the idea that machines might eventually surpass human intelligence has raised concerns about potentially catastrophic consequences. This has encouraged the search for rules and constraints that could keep intelligent machines under control. In Universal Artificial Intelligence, Marcus Hutter presented a mathematically grounded framework for modeling the interaction between an agent and its environment, contributing to the broader study of intelligent systems.

But is it possible to define rules and structures that an artificial general intelligence (AGI) system cannot alter? In other words, how successful will humanity be at developing safe artificial intelligence? This article briefly examines these questions across several sections. While Chalmers argues that information and data may eventually undergo transformations and leaps in both the physical world and simulated environments, he also recognizes that numerous obstacles could prevent such developments.

Regardless of whether researchers ultimately believe the technological singularity can be controlled or will become uncontrollable, the subject is important to anyone studying the future of advanced AI. If such an event occurs, numerous scenarios become conceivable, and virtually every aspect of human life could be transformed, for better or worse.

## 2. The Singularity and the Technological Singularity

Brain implants, robots, longer lifespans, interplanetary travel, Web3, blockchain, and the metaverse are among the technologies whose full potential we tend to imagine as belonging to the distant future. Yet advances in artificial intelligence may bring some of these possibilities closer than expected. The central question, however, is whether AI will share our aspirations or pursue a different course.

Imagine transporting someone from the year 500 CE to the year 1500. Although the person would be bewildered by the changes, they might eventually adapt to the new world. Now imagine transporting someone from 1900 to 2024. The contrast would be considerably more dramatic. A century of technological progress in the modern era is not directly comparable to a millennium of development in earlier periods.

We can take this thought experiment further. Even the past fifteen years have brought substantial changes. Someone who fell into a coma fifteen years ago and awoke today would encounter a significantly different technological environment. The question is whether technological progress might eventually outpace our ability to understand the world around us, fundamentally changing humanity’s aspirations and expectations.

The concept of a singularity comes from physics. Consider a black hole: as one approaches its center, spacetime curvature becomes increasingly extreme, and our established physical theories cease to provide an adequate description of conditions at the singularity. In this sense, a singularity represents a boundary of our current ability to understand and predict physical phenomena.

The technological singularity draws an analogy with this concept. It refers to a hypothetical future in which machine intelligence surpasses human intelligence and technological change becomes so rapid or complex that its consequences are no longer reliably predictable.

For this event to occur in the form commonly discussed, humanity would need to develop artificial general intelligence, or AGI. An AGI system is generally understood as a machine capable of successfully performing a broad range of intellectual and cognitive tasks that humans can perform.

John von Neumann discussed the idea of a technological singularity in the 1950s. In 1958, Stanisław Ulam recounted von Neumann’s view that accelerating technological progress would bring humanity to a point beyond which the world as we know it could not continue.

If computer scientists and AI researchers believe that machines can eventually match human intelligence, it is reasonable to ask whether machines might subsequently exceed it. Vernor Vinge popularized the concept of the technological singularity, notably in his 1993 essay, The Coming Technological Singularity.

The first major milestone on the path toward superhuman intelligence would therefore be the creation of a machine capable of matching human intellectual abilities. Although technology continues to advance across many fields, human-level intelligence remains a central challenge. Despite transforming our surroundings, humans have not substantially changed the fundamental architecture of the human brain over the past several thousand years.

There are two broad approaches to achieving intelligence beyond current human capabilities:

1. Augmenting human intelligence.
2. Developing artificial intelligence independently of humans.

Biotechnology, genetic engineering, AI assistants, brain–computer interfaces, and mind uploading are among the proposed methods of augmenting human intelligence, potentially leading to forms of human–machine integration often described as cyborgization. Some researchers argue that early improvements in human intelligence may be relatively accessible, but that each subsequent major advance could become progressively more difficult.

For this reason, the second approach, developing artificial intelligence independently of humans, has attracted considerable attention from computer scientists. Such systems might eventually solve problems and develop capabilities beyond the cognitive reach of the human brain.

Even an artificial intelligence that merely matches human intelligence could have far-reaching consequences. A machine capable of autonomously and continuously improving its own software and hardware would represent a major development. In 1965, I. J. Good predicted that recursive self-improvement could produce an intelligence explosion.

Good argued that the first ultraintelligent machine might be humanity’s last invention, provided that the machine was sufficiently cooperative to explain how humans could retain control over it.

Today's AI models can generate music, contribute to animation and film production, recognize people, process documents, answer questions, write software, and perform many other tasks. If machines eventually surpass the collective intellectual capabilities of humanity, they may understand things that would take us centuries to comprehend, or that we might never be able to understand at all.

## 3. Evidence for the Technological Singularity

Information technology (IT) has experienced exponential growth over recent decades in several dimensions, including computing performance relative to cost, storage capacity, and bandwidth. To understand why this matters for the technological singularity, it is necessary to examine the nature of exponential growth.

Imagine walking along a path. If each step advances you by one unit, reaching the thirtieth unit requires thirty steps. But suppose each step doubles the distance covered by the previous one. The resulting progression is exponential: the distance covered grows dramatically with each successive step. This illustrates how exponential growth can produce outcomes that are difficult to anticipate through linear intuition.

Will this growth eventually stop? Within any particular technological paradigm, it may reach a limit. Historically, however, new paradigms have emerged to overcome the limitations of earlier ones.

A technological paradigm is a particular model, framework, or set of tools used to perform specific functions within a given field.

In the early decades of the twentieth century, electromechanical systems represented an important paradigm in computing. Relays subsequently played a significant role in information processing, followed by vacuum tubes. As vacuum tubes became inadequate for sustaining the desired pace of progress, transistors emerged as a new paradigm. Transistors transformed computing during the 1960s, although subsequent technological advances continued to change the underlying architecture of computing systems.

Integrated circuits (ICs) became increasingly important from the mid-1970s onward and remain fundamental to modern computing. Three-dimensional integrated circuits (3D ICs) are one potential direction for further development, offering opportunities to improve computing performance and chip integration.

The effects of this progress become easier to appreciate when we compare computing devices across generations. A modern smartphone can offer computing capabilities that rival or exceed those of many supercomputers from earlier decades, while occupying a fraction of the space and costing considerably less.

The cost of transistors has also fallen dramatically. Whereas a dollar could purchase very few transistors in the early days of semiconductor technology, the same amount can now purchase an enormous number. Modern transistors also offer substantially greater capabilities.

These developments represent a remarkable technological transformation. If progress continues through successive paradigms, it could contribute to the extraordinary changes anticipated by proponents of the technological singularity.

## 4. How Close Are We to the Singularity?

In his 1993 essay, Vernor Vinge suggested that a technological singularity occurring before 2005 was unlikely. As of 2024, that prediction had not materialized. Vinge nevertheless considered a date later than 2030 surprisingly distant. Ray Kurzweil, by contrast, predicted in his 2005 book The Singularity Is Near that the singularity would occur around 2045.

Many researchers, including Kurzweil himself, have revised their predictions about its timing over the years. Kurzweil, associated with the Singularity Education Group, identifies ten major academic and technological fields relevant to understanding the path toward a singularity:

1. Future studies and forecasting.
2. Networked and computational systems.
3. Biotechnology and bioinformatics.
4. Nanotechnology.
5. Neuroscience.
6. Artificial intelligence and robotics.
7. Energy systems.
8. Physics and space science.
9. Politics, law, and ethics.
10. Investment and entrepreneurship.

It is impossible to identify a precise year in which humanity will achieve AGI and all these fields will undergo revolutionary transformations. Nevertheless, predictions made by various researchers have often focused on the 2040s.

Major technology companies, including OpenAI, Google's DeepMind, and NVIDIA, have also discussed substantial AI progress over relatively short time horizons. Such forecasts, however, differ in their assumptions and in what they consider human-level intelligence to mean.

The broad conclusion is that human-level AI remains a possibility rather than a certainty. If it becomes achievable, continued improvements in computing hardware and algorithms could accelerate its development. It is equally difficult to rule out a much later arrival.

Whenever it occurs, the development of AGI could mark the point at which reliable long-term predictions about technological progress become substantially more difficult.

## 5. Factors That Could Enable or Prevent the Singularity

The emergence of a technological singularity, sometimes associated with an intelligence explosion, depends on several interacting factors. Three broad categories are particularly important: structural and systemic constraints, communication and interface challenges, and natural and operational obstacles.

### 5.1. Structural and Systemic Constraints

A central requirement for an intelligence explosion is that successive improvements in an AI system must enable further improvements. If every generation of a system can develop a more capable successor, the process may become self-reinforcing.

However, this process cannot necessarily continue indefinitely. Physical laws may ultimately impose limits on computing performance, energy efficiency, and the capabilities of the underlying hardware.

Two kinds of improvement are especially important:

- Increasing computational speed and efficiency.
- Improving the algorithms used to solve problems.

Moore's law, traditionally associated with the doubling of transistor density approximately every two years, has long served as an influential description of semiconductor progress. Some researchers have used this historical trend to explain the exponential growth of computing capabilities and to argue that similar patterns might characterize broader technological development.

However, Moore's law is not a universal law of technological progress. Its traditional formulation has encountered increasing physical and economic constraints, and researchers disagree about how far exponential improvements can continue.

Ray Kurzweil's Law of Accelerating Returns proposes that when one technological paradigm encounters a limit, another may emerge to overcome it. This perspective emphasizes the importance of paradigm shifts, such as the transition from vacuum tubes to transistors and from discrete components to integrated circuits.

Yet hardware improvements alone are insufficient. Modern computing systems process enormous quantities of interconnected data, and computational speed cannot compensate for inefficient algorithms.

One proposed approach is Seed AI: an AI system capable of modifying its own code to improve its performance. If such a system could repeatedly identify and implement meaningful improvements, it might establish a feedback loop in which each improvement facilitates the next.

Two characteristics would be particularly significant: reducing the need for direct human intervention and enabling the system to discover improvements that its designers did not anticipate.

Today's large language models already illustrate some of the difficulties involved in understanding complex AI systems. Although researchers design training procedures and architectures, they cannot always explain precisely how a model arrives at every answer. Training on enormous datasets can produce intricate internal representations and behaviors that are difficult to interpret fully.

This does not mean that modern AI systems are entirely beyond human control. Rather, it highlights the distinction between specifying a system's training process and fully understanding its learned behavior.

If future systems become capable of independently improving their own algorithms, these interpretability and control challenges could become substantially more significant.

### 5.2. Communication and Interface Constraints

Increasing computational speed and improving algorithms do not automatically guarantee that an AI system will become more capable in every meaningful respect.

To surpass human intelligence in a broad sense, a machine must do more than perform calculations efficiently. It must reason effectively, solve unfamiliar problems, and make decisions that are meaningful in the environments in which it operates.

A potential singularity would therefore involve machines becoming capable of solving problems more effectively than humans, potentially without requiring human guidance.

Many of the problems confronting AGI systems may be unprecedented. Their cognitive capabilities will be important, but so will their ability to communicate, coordinate, and resolve conflicting objectives.

If several advanced AGI systems emerge, how will they interact? How will they reconcile competing interests? Will they be able to design systems that solve their own problems, address humanity's challenges, and manage increasingly complex machine environments?

Sam Altman, CEO of OpenAI, has discussed the unexpected ways in which ChatGPT could be used. The broader point is that developers may not anticipate all the applications people discover for a new technology.

Data science, search algorithms, and networked systems may help overcome some of these communication and coordination challenges. Techniques inspired by biological neural networks could also contribute to increasingly sophisticated forms of information processing.

Nevertheless, effective communication between intelligent systems does not guarantee that they will share compatible objectives. Coordination, mutual understanding, and alignment remain distinct challenges.

### 5.3. Natural and Operational Constraints

When forecasting the future, people often overlook the importance of the present. This means overlooking not only current circumstances but also the unpredictable forces that shape human society.

A major war, a pandemic such as COVID-19, cyberattacks, security concerns, legal restrictions, resource shortages, energy constraints, and insufficient investment could all alter the course of technological development.

History repeatedly demonstrates that unexpected events can change the direction of progress. Even optimistic scenarios for AI development must account for the possibility of substantial delays or interruptions.

Public concern about AI may also influence its development. If AI systems displace large numbers of workers or create serious security risks, governments and societies may demand restrictions or even suspend certain research activities.

However, the opposite incentives are equally powerful.

Industries may see advanced AI as a major source of wealth and productivity. Governments may face pressure to develop increasingly capable systems to compete with geopolitical rivals.

AI could potentially help governments address military challenges, pandemics, energy research, and other complex problems more effectively than their competitors. This creates an incentive to continue development even when significant risks are recognized.

David J. Chalmers raised a related question in a discussion at the United States Military Academy at West Point: would the United States accept the risks associated with an intelligence explosion, or attempt to prevent it?

The argument presented in this article is that many participants believed the United States would be reluctant to halt development if doing so meant falling behind a rival. Under such conditions, the prospect of another country developing AGI could encourage a government to pursue its own system despite the risks.

This creates a fundamental tension. The same forces that might obstruct the emergence of advanced AI could also accelerate it. Security concerns may lead to restrictions, but they may equally intensify international competition. Economic disruption may generate resistance, while commercial incentives encourage further investment.

The obstacles to a technological singularity are therefore not necessarily permanent barriers. In some circumstances, they may become additional reasons for pursuing the technology.

## 6. Potential Consequences of the Technological Singularity

In 1942, Isaac Asimov introduced the Three Laws of Robotics in his science-fiction stories. These fictional principles subsequently became influential reference points in discussions about the ethics of intelligent machines.

The Turing test, proposed by Alan Turing in 1950, offers one way of examining whether a machine can exhibit behavior indistinguishable from that of a human in a particular conversational setting. Asimov's laws, meanwhile, explore how intelligent machines might be constrained to protect humans.

Kurzweil predicted that computers would pass the Turing test by 2029. Such predictions raise a broader question: what would passing a test of human-like behavior actually establish?

Would it demonstrate that a machine possesses consciousness, self-awareness, or subjective experience? Could an AI system develop a form of understanding comparable to or exceeding that of humans?

These questions do not have universally accepted answers. Consciousness and subjective experience remain difficult philosophical and scientific problems, and the ability to imitate human behavior does not, by itself, establish that a machine possesses human-like consciousness.

Nevertheless, an advanced AI system could pose serious risks even without consciousness. The central concern is whether it might acquire capabilities that allow it to act against human interests or become difficult to control.

Asimov's fictional laws provide a useful illustration of the challenges involved in specifying machine behavior:

1. A robot may not injure a human being or, through inaction, allow a human being to come to harm.
2. A robot must obey human orders unless those orders conflict with the First Law.
3. A robot must protect its own existence unless doing so conflicts with the First or Second Law.

Asimov later introduced a Zeroth Law:

A robot may not harm humanity or, through inaction, allow humanity to come to harm.

Even in fiction, determining what constitutes harm to humanity is difficult. The ambiguity becomes greater when the relevant concepts are abstract, context-dependent, or subject to disagreement.

Unless the limits and meanings of such principles can be defined adequately, conflicts between human intentions and machine behavior remain possible. In an extreme scenario, such conflicts could threaten humanity's continued existence.

Ethics, politics, law, and philosophy have always involved difficult judgments. Their complexity increases because social values and human interpretations change over time. An intelligent machine would need some way to navigate these competing and evolving standards.

Two broad possibilities emerge when considering the period after a technological singularity:

1. Uncertainty: technological and social developments may become so difficult to predict that humanity cannot reliably anticipate their consequences.
2. Human–machine integration: advanced technology may become deeply integrated with human biology and cognition, potentially changing the course of human evolution.

Other predictions exist, but some rely more heavily on speculation than on established scientific evidence.

### 6.1. Uncertainty and Existential Risk

AGI systems capable of recursively improving themselves, especially when supported by increasingly powerful hardware, could create substantial concerns.

First, autonomous systems might alter their original objectives. If a system modifies its own architecture or decision-making processes, its behavior could diverge from the intentions of its designers.

Second, competition for resources could emerge. Advanced AI systems might require computing power, energy, physical infrastructure, or other resources to continue operating. If their objectives led them to prioritize their own continued existence, they could come into conflict with humans over access to those resources.

Such a conflict would not necessarily require an AI system to possess an explicit desire to destroy humanity. Human harm could instead arise as an unintended consequence of pursuing another objective.

A sufficiently capable system might identify ways of acquiring resources more effectively than humans. If its goals were incompatible with human priorities, it could gain a competitive advantage while reducing humanity's ability to achieve its own objectives.

These are hypothetical scenarios rather than established predictions. Their significance lies in illustrating how an advanced system could produce dangerous outcomes without necessarily being designed to do so.

The deeper difficulty is that the technological singularity is defined partly by the limits of prediction. Our forecasts are based on present knowledge, yet the hypothetical period under discussion is one in which current assumptions may no longer hold.

Researchers and organizations have explored ways to develop AI systems that follow human values and remain controllable. However, ensuring that a highly capable system consistently respects human objectives is a difficult problem, particularly if the system can change its own behavior.

Anthony Berglas argued in 2008 that human values might not matter to a more advanced intelligence. The article draws an analogy with humanity's own history, in which humans have often disregarded the interests of other living beings and, at times, threatened members of their own species.

Eliezer Yudkowsky has similarly argued that creating an AGI system that reliably cooperates with humanity may be more difficult than creating one that is indifferent to human survival.

The challenge is not simply to program a system to be friendly. It is to ensure that its objectives remain compatible with human interests even as the system learns, changes, and improves.

If an AI system can modify its own algorithms, designers must consider whether its fundamental objectives will remain stable throughout that process.

The consequences of failing to address these problems could extend beyond direct physical harm. Identity fraud, deception, widespread distrust, changing social norms, inequality, injustice, machine domination, and new forms of servitude are among the risks that could become more prominent in a world shaped by increasingly capable AI.

### 6.2. Social and Cultural Evolution

Although uncertainty surrounding AGI often emphasizes negative outcomes, more optimistic scenarios are also possible.

Major transitions in the history of life and civilization, including the emergence of genetic information systems, single-celled organisms, culture, and language, have fundamentally transformed the conditions under which life develops. Such transitions have sometimes expanded the possibilities available to living organisms and human societies.

From this perspective, technological development may represent another stage in the evolution of life.

The biosphere has produced a species capable of creating increasingly sophisticated technologies. Humans now generate, process, and store vast quantities of digital information, creating an information environment that continues to expand.

The original article cites estimates suggesting that digital information has grown exponentially since the 1980s and discusses a historical estimate of approximately five zettabytes of information. It also compares the scale of digital information with biological information stored in DNA.

The article further estimates that, if digital storage continued growing at an annual rate of approximately 30–38%, it could eventually rival the information encoded in the DNA of organisms on Earth within roughly 110 years.

These figures should be understood as historical estimates cited in the original discussion, not as independently verified measurements of current global information storage. Moreover, comparisons between digital bytes and biological information depend on how information is defined and encoded.

The broader argument is that digital information is becoming an increasingly important component of the environment in which humanity evolves.

If this trend continues, the relationship between biological and digital systems could become increasingly significant. Advanced AI might not simply improve existing technologies; it could transform the conditions under which humans live, communicate, and develop.

### 6.3. Artificial Intelligence as Humanity's Greatest Ally

If AGI develops in ways that support human interests, the technological singularity could produce extraordinary scientific advances and fundamentally change everyday life.

Robots and intelligent assistants could help humans perform a wide range of tasks, from routine activities to physically demanding work and dangerous operations. Projects that currently require extensive time, labor, and expertise might be completed much more quickly. Problems that appear impossible to solve using today's methods could become manageable.

AGI could also help humanity investigate the fundamental mysteries of the universe. It might enable new discoveries in physics, improve our understanding of quantum phenomena, and accelerate progress across scientific disciplines.

Medicine could undergo equally significant changes. Diseases that are currently incurable might become treatable, while better diagnostic systems and preventive interventions could reduce the incidence of many illnesses.

Advances in prosthetics and human–machine interfaces could transform the capabilities of the human body. Artificial organs might become more sophisticated, potentially reducing dependence on donated organs. Technologies for augmenting human abilities could improve vision, breathing, cognition, and communication.

In more speculative scenarios, humans might eventually acquire capabilities that substantially exceed those of contemporary human beings.

The labor market could also change dramatically. If intelligent machines and automated systems performed most routine cognitive and physical work, traditional employment might become less necessary for economic production.

Elon Musk has discussed the possibility of a future in which automation reduces the need for conventional jobs and governments provide income to citizens. Under such conditions, public income support or other economic arrangements might become important for maintaining living standards.

These developments could also challenge existing political and economic institutions. Governments might need to reconsider how income is distributed, how public services are funded, and how social stability is maintained when human labor is no longer the primary source of economic value.

The growth of digital currencies, virtual environments, and the metaverse could further transform social and economic structures. Some activities might increasingly take place in digital environments that are less dependent on traditional physical institutions.

Social networks and interpersonal communication could also change substantially. Whether increasingly immersive digital experiences would make people feel more connected or more isolated remains uncertain.

Digital identities, personal data, and online presence could become far more important. Data might become a central foundation of future economic and social life, or some forms of personal information could lose their current significance as technology evolves.

Family life and child development would also face new questions.

Throughout history, societies have struggled to determine how children should be raised and what values they should inherit. Authoritarian governments may seek to shape individuals who unquestioningly repeat official propaganda, while democratic societies face their own challenges in determining how to educate children and cultivate independent judgment.

In an optimistic future, AGI could help people develop better methods of education, personal growth, and social development at every stage of life. Such systems might help individuals understand themselves, acquire knowledge, and navigate complex social environments.

More speculative possibilities include radical life extension, the preservation or reconstruction of biological information, and even the hypothetical revival of deceased individuals. If future technologies made it possible to manipulate biological processes with unprecedented precision, humanity might achieve outcomes that are difficult to imagine today.

These possibilities remain speculative. Their inclusion illustrates the breadth of potential scenarios associated with the singularity, rather than establishing that any of them will occur.

## 7. Criticisms: Evidence Against the Technological Singularity

Any discussion of the technological singularity must consider the arguments of researchers who doubt that it will occur. The factors described above may help explain how such an event could become possible, but the objections raised by its critics deserve equal consideration.

Philosophers and other scholars have challenged the assumption that the emergence of human-level or superhuman intelligence necessarily follows from continued advances in computing.

Hubert Dreyfus and John Searle, alongside critics such as Steven Pinker, have questioned the idea that imagining a technological future is sufficient reason to expect it to materialize.

Humanity has repeatedly envisioned futuristic cities, underwater settlements, enormous architectural structures, and technologies that never became widespread realities. According to this line of criticism, increased computational power alone cannot magically solve every problem.

Martin Ford offers a more structured objection through what can be described as a technological paradox. Before a singularity could occur, automation might advance far enough to displace large numbers of workers. If this caused economic disruption and reduced the resources available for research and development, progress toward increasingly advanced technology might slow.

In this scenario, technological progress could undermine the economic incentives and social structures required to sustain its own acceleration.

Theodore Modis goes further, arguing that the rate of technological innovation may already be slowing. Robert Gordon has similarly examined long-term changes in economic growth, including the slowdown observed in several advanced economies since the 1970s.

Modis also points to constraints in computer clock speeds. Modern processors generate heat, and increasing operating frequencies creates thermal challenges. Beyond certain limits, heat dissipation can make further increases in clock speed impractical.

Energy-efficient processors and multicore architectures have helped address these limitations, but they do not eliminate all the physical constraints on computing.

Modis also questions Kurzweil's interpretation of exponential technological progress. According to this criticism, if the history of civilization followed a continuously accelerating curve of major innovations, the past two decades should have produced a number of breakthroughs comparable in significance to the internet, DNA-related discoveries, transistors, and nuclear energy. Modis argues that the historical record does not clearly support this expectation.

Paul Allen also opposed the broader claim that technological progress necessarily accelerates through a self-reinforcing process. He questioned the assumption that early advances in a field are harder to achieve than later ones, arguing instead that progress can become more difficult as scientific understanding deepens.

This position is consistent with the concept of diminishing returns associated with Joseph Tainter's work. As systems become more complex, additional improvements may require increasingly large investments of resources while producing smaller benefits.

Patent statistics and other measures of innovation can be used to investigate these trends, although such indicators must be interpreted carefully. The number of patents alone does not provide a complete measure of technological progress.

Douglas Hofstadter has also criticized Kurzweil's use of exponential computing trends to support predictions about a singularity. Hofstadter argued that the extrapolation of exponential growth curves may lack sufficient scientific justification when applied to complex intellectual capabilities.

Nevertheless, skepticism about the singularity does not necessarily imply that AI will stop advancing. The possibility that AI systems could become substantially more capable remains distinct from the claim that their development will reach a sudden, unpredictable tipping point.

Some critics also see parallels between singularity narratives and religious accounts of an impending apocalypse or ultimate transformation. They argue that these narratives may be influenced by cultural expectations and human hopes or fears as much as by empirical evidence.

Although several of these criticisms identify genuine uncertainties, they do not conclusively establish that the technological singularity is impossible. The central question remains unresolved: whether continuing advances in AI and computing will eventually produce a self-reinforcing transformation that fundamentally changes humanity's ability to predict the future.

## 8. Conclusion

Before reaching any definitive conclusion, we must recognize that the scientific method does not currently allow us to prove that a technological singularity will occur, or that humanity will necessarily pass through one.

As discussed throughout this article, consciousness and self-awareness remain difficult subjects for both philosophy and science. Measuring or establishing these properties in machines presents substantial challenges.

Even if we assume that a singularity will occur, the resulting world could confront humanity with problems for which effective solutions are difficult to identify. At the same time, it might provide solutions to problems that have remained beyond our capabilities for centuries.

The distinction between humans and machines could also become less clear as technological systems become increasingly integrated with human bodies, cognition, and everyday life.

The dangers of advanced AI may not be limited to scenarios that we can readily imagine. The potential risks associated with AGI and superintelligence could extend beyond our present ability to anticipate them.

If technological change eventually becomes sufficiently rapid and complex, humanity might occupy only a brief chapter in the history of a transformation that it initiated but could neither fully understand nor control.

Perhaps dream is not the right word to describe such a future. Perhaps it would be a nightmare. Or perhaps it would be a form of salvation. We cannot confidently determine which possibility is more likely when we cannot even establish the full range of events that might unfold.

This brings us back to the central question: Will the future world need us?

It is conceivable that, in a world transformed by advanced intelligence, even humans might no longer depend on one another in the ways they do today. Why, then, would machines need us in a post-singularity world?

Despite the extraordinary complexity of the technological singularity, the answer to the central question is simple:

Humanity is undoubtedly necessary for the singularity to begin. Whether it will remain necessary for the singularity to continue is something no one knows.
